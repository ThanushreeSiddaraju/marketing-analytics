Where Lumora's ad money went in 2025, and where it should go next

Lumora is a made-up B2B SaaS with three plans and 19 campaigns across Google, Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok, email and affiliates. I generated a year of ads, website visits, CRM stages and invoices for it, cleaned it in DuckDB, and worked through it the way a growth team would. All numbers below come from that generated data, except one section near the end that analyses a real email experiment.

What went into this

I built this end to end, from generating and cleaning the data to the models, the agents and this page. Each part below links to its code.

SQL and data modelling

Raw exports go into DuckDB and come out as a star schema: five fact tables and four dimensions. The staging SQL deduplicates with QUALIFY ROW_NUMBER(), maps messy channel names, and fixes impossible values. The ten analysis queries use window functions, UNPIVOT, FILTER and LAG to work out funnel drop-off.

sql/

Marketing analytics

CAC, ROAS, LTV to CAC, payback period, churn, funnel conversion, creative and device performance, and four rule-based attribution models. Each one answers a question a growth team actually asks, like where the next dollar should go or which step loses the most people.

sql/analysis/

Attribution modelling

Markov chain removal effects and exact Shapley values, written from scratch rather than with a library. They're tested against small cases worked out by hand and against the SQL rule-based numbers, which they match exactly.

src/attribution.py

Machine learning

Lead scoring with XGBoost and logistic regression, using only what's known when the form is filled in so nothing leaks from the future. It's validated month by month and explained with SHAP, with calibration and lift checks. When the simple model did as well as the complex one, I said so.

src/lead_scoring.py

Mix modelling and optimisation

Adstock and saturation curves fitted with weak priors, plus a block bootstrap for uncertainty. I checked parameter recovery against a simulation where the true effects are known. A constrained budget optimiser runs on top, and its promise is scored against the true curves too.

src/mmm.py

Experiments and statistics

A real randomised test with 64,000 customers. The analysis covers a sample ratio check, balance checks, Welch intervals, a Holm correction, CUPED, power and sample size, effects by customer group, and a targeting rule tested on held-out customers.

src/hillstrom.py

LLM agents

Four agents on the Anthropic SDK using tool use and structured outputs. SQL access is locked down twice, by a parser and by DuckDB itself. Every number an agent states cites a query and is re-checked in code before anyone sees it. A reviewer agent and a 32-question eval with known answers round it off.

src/agents/

Engineering

A reproducible data generator with problems planted on purpose, a data contract that rejects broken files before loading, 73 pytest tests, a ruff lint step, CI on two Python versions, and GitHub Actions that rebuild the data, rerun every model and publish this site on every push. The real dataset downloads with a checksum check and is cached in CI.

Cost per customer by channel

Email is cheap because the list already exists. Of the paid channels, Google Search is the most efficient. Display and TikTok took 17% of the budget and brought in 12 of 623 customers, which makes them the easiest money to move. LinkedIn costs twice as much per customer as Search, but those customers pick bigger plans and churn at about half the rate, so its lifetime value to cost ratio is the best of any paid channel.

The funnel

Most people never click, which is normal for ads, and only a few percent of visits turn into leads. Once someone is a lead, the biggest drop is the last step: only about a quarter of sales-qualified leads end up paying. That's a sales and pricing question more than a marketing one.

Month by month

Subscriptions pay monthly, so revenue keeps climbing as customers stack up, while spend stays roughly flat apart from the spring launch and Black Friday. Revenue passed monthly ad spend in April and was more than twice it by December.

Free-trial ads get the most clicks and convert best once people land; ebooks do worst. Mobile clicks are cheap but convert at about half the desktop rate, so a mobile customer costs nearly twice as much. The mobile sign-up form is the first thing I'd look at.

Who gets credit

Last touch gives all the credit to the final click before someone signs up. The Markov chain and Shapley models look at whole journeys instead. They agree with each other closely, and both move credit towards Meta and Display, which tend to start journeys that Search, email and direct visits finish. Display still doesn't pay for itself, but it's closer to breaking even than last touch makes it look.

A real campaign: the Hillstrom email test

Everything above uses generated data, so I also wanted something real. In 2008 Kevin Hillstrom published a test from an online retailer: 64,000 recent customers were randomly sent an email about men's merchandise, an email about women's merchandise, or nothing, and their visits, purchases and spend were tracked for the next two weeks. Because the groups were picked at random, the gap between each email group and the no-email group is what the email caused.

What the cleaning step fixed

I put problems into the raw data on purpose, the kind that turn up in real exports, and the staging SQL fixes them. The tests check that none of them make it into the final tables.

Days the numbers looked wrong

Cleaning catches rows that are impossible on their own. Some problems only show up over time, like a tracking pixel that stops firing for a week. So for each paid channel and day I compare click-through rate, CPM and impressions per dollar with the median of the previous four weeks, and flag anything more than four robust standard deviations out. It found all three problems I'd planted, plus a handful of one-day blips, which is about what noise looks like at this threshold.

It also caught a mistake of mine. My first cleaning rule capped clicks at impressions for the rows that reported more clicks than impressions, which still left those rows at a 100% click rate, and nearly every one-day spike the check flagged landed on one of them. Those clicks are now re-estimated from the same ad's normal click rate.

Agents

There are also four LLM agents built on the Anthropic SDK: an analyst that answers questions by writing SQL, a reviewer that checks the SQL answers the question that was asked, a data quality agent that reads the raw tables, and a writer that turns checked answers into a short brief.

None of their numbers are taken on trust. The SQL tool only runs a single read-only SELECT, checked by a parser and again by DuckDB's own lockdown. Every number an agent states has to cite the query it came from, and code re-runs that query to make sure the number is really there. The writer only sees answers that passed, and every number in its text is matched back to a checked value. A 32-question eval with known answers measures how often the analyst gets it right. The details are in the README.
